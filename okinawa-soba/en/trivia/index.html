🌾 Called “soba,” but zero buckwheat

Okinawa soba noodles are made from wheat flour. Despite the name “soba,” they contain no buckwheat at all.

The dough is made by adding salt and kansui (alkaline water) — or lye from wood ash — to wheat flour, so the method is closer to Chinese-style noodles.

Traditionally, the boiled noodles are coated with oil and left to cool. Recently, more shops serve noodles without the oil coating.

📜 The history of Okinawa soba

Noodle dishes are said to have come from China, but there are several theories about when, and it is not clear.

The oldest soba shop that can be confirmed in records dates to April 1902 (Meiji 35), when the Ryukyu Shimpo newspaper ran an opening advertisement for a “Shina Soba-ya” in Naha. Back then it was called “shina soba,” “tojin soba,” or simply “suba,” and the soup is said to have been dark and soy-sauce based.

In the Taisho era, fish cake appeared as a topping, and a clear soup using less soy sauce and more salt was introduced. Shops such as “Yutaka-ya” in Shuri and “Ushinma Soba” in Tsuji are said to have shaped the Okinawa soba we know today.

Many soba shops were lost in the Battle of Okinawa, but after the war soba-making revived using wheat flour distributed by the U.S. military. It is said that many women who had lost their husbands in the war opened soba shops to make a living.

The name “Okinawa soba” became widespread only after the war.

📅 October 17 is “Okinawa Soba Day”

In 1976 (Showa 51), after Okinawa's reversion to Japan, the Japan Fair Trade Commission pointed out that the noodles could not be labeled “soba” because they contained no buckwheat. Under the nationwide rule, only noodles made with at least 30% buckwheat flour could be called “soba.”

With the name “Okinawa soba” in danger of disappearing, Kenichi Dohi, then chairman of the Okinawa Fresh Noodle Cooperative Association, and others kept negotiating persistently. On October 17, 1978 (Showa 53), the name “Honba Okinawa Soba” (authentic Okinawa soba) was specially approved.

To commemorate that day, the association designated October 17 as “Okinawa Soba Day” in 1997 (Heisei 9).

Incidentally, “Honba Okinawa Soba” is said to have detailed rules — it must be made in Okinawa Prefecture, made in the hand-made style, hand-kneaded before boiling, and so on.

🌳 Mokkai (wood-ash) soba

Instead of kansui, these noodles are made using lye — the clear liquid taken from water in which the ash of burned wood, such as banyan (gajumaru), has been soaked.

This was the norm before the war, but afterwards gas became common and ash became hard to obtain, so kansui became the mainstream.

Shops that serve labor-intensive mokkai soba are now rare. It has a distinctive flavor and texture, and a loyal following.

🥢 Noodles and noodle makers

Okinawa soba noodles come in two ways: house-made noodles made at the shop, and noodles bought in from a noodle factory (seimenjo). Knowing whose noodles a shop uses makes eating around even more fun.

Noodles vary in shape: thin, medium-thick, thick and flat, as well as straight and curly. According to the Okinawa Fresh Noodle Cooperative Association, it is said that flat, thicker noodles are common in the north of the main island, thick noodles in the central area, and thin noodles in the south. Recently, though, noodles are reportedly chosen more and more by customers’ taste rather than by region.

Here are the noodle makers whose names you often hear when eating around.

  • Terukina Seimenjo (Naha City): known for strongly curly noodles. A standard choice used by many soba shops in Okinawa.
  • Kamehama Seimenjo (Tomigusuku City): thin, straight noodles with a firm bite. It is said to have operated for many years in Tsuji, Naha. The two are sometimes mentioned side by side: “Terukina for curly noodles, Kamehama for thin noodles.”
  • Chinen Seimenjo (Okinawa City): founded in 1955 (Showa 30). It is said to keep making noodles by hand, without making them ahead of time.
  • Nishizaki Seimenjo (Itoman City): besides noodles kneaded with mugwort, squid ink or mozuku seaweed, it also makes original noodles for individual soba shops.
  • Sankakuya Seimenjo (Nago City): a long-established maker known for the flat noodles of “Nago soba.” Many soba shops in Nago are said to use its noodles.
  • Sun Foods (Itoman City): the company founded by Kenichi Dohi, who appears in the story of Okinawa Soba Day. It also makes Okinawa soba for souvenirs, such as semi-fresh noodles that keep well.

Some soba shops are run directly by a noodle maker. The appeal is eating freshly made noodles right there.

At shops with house-made noodles, you can find noodles you can only eat there — made with Okinawan wheat or whole wheat flour, or kneaded with mugwort. The first rating on this blog is “noodles” because they matter that much.

🏮 Tojin soba

This is the Meiji-era taste said to be the root of Okinawa soba. It is said to have been a simple bowl: a dark soy-sauce soup topped only with pork and green onion.

In 2018, the Okinawa Soba Development and Succession Association recreated it over about ten months based on old records, and it can now be eaten at shops in the prefecture.

April 9 is designated as “Tojin Soba Day.”

🐟 About the broth

The base is a blend of pork-bone and bonito (katsuobushi) broth. Some shops lean on bonito, some make the pork bone rich, and some add kelp or chicken bones — this is where a shop's character shows most.

That is why this blog always starts with “First, the soup.”

Incidentally, Naha City ranked first in Japan for the amount of bonito flakes purchased in the government's household survey (2013–2015 average) — an overwhelming first place at more than six times the national average. It has also been reported that Naha ranked first in spending on bonito flakes for ten straight years, from 2013 to 2022. Okinawans really do love bonito broth.

🥩 About the toppings

  • Sanmai-niku: skin-on pork belly simmered sweet and savory. When you say “Okinawa soba,” this comes first.
  • Soki: pork spare ribs. There is “hon-soki” with hard bones, and “nankotsu soki” (soft-bone ribs) that you can eat bones and all.
  • Tebichi: slowly simmered pig's feet.
  • Kōrēgūsu: a table condiment of island chili peppers steeped in awamori. Be careful not to add too much.

Soki soba is said to have originated at “Gabusoka Shokudo” in Nago City. In 1966 (Showa 41), it reportedly began when soki, which tended to be left over at the butcher shop, was served on top of soba.

🗾 Okinawa soba differs by region

Even within Okinawa, the noodles and presentation differ from region to region.

  • Miyako soba: the traditional presentation hides the toppings under the noodles.
  • Yaeyama soba: known for thin, round, straight noodles. Thinly sliced fish cake is the standard topping.
  • Northern main island (Yanbaru): around Nago, my impression is that many shops serve flat noodles.
